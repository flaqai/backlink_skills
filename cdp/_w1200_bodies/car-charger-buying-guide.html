The car charger is the most-abused gadget most people own: baked in summer sun, frozen in winter, yanked by the cable, and blamed for phones that charge "slow." It is also a ten-dollar decision that affects your daily battery top-up, so it deserves two minutes of thought instead of a checkout-aisle grab. This guide covers what actually matters in a car charger — ports, wattage, build — and what to ignore. For measured performance of specific models, the car and wall charger reviews at [SPRAVS](https://Spravs) test output under real conditions.

What Matters

Total wattage, honestly stated. A car charger with two USB-C ports and "100W" on the box is splitting that across ports; with both in use, your phone might see 30W. For single-device charging, anything from 20W to 45W covers modern phones comfortably. For a phone plus a passenger's phone, 45W to 65W total keeps both happy. Only laptops-in-the-car scenarios need 100W-class units — and a car is a bad laptop office anyway.

USB-C Power Delivery support. This is the single feature to insist on. PD is the negotiation protocol iPhones and Androids use for fast charging; without it, even a high-amp charger delivers slow speeds. A 25W-plus PD port charges an iPhone at near-full speed and most Androids at or near theirs.

A second port, ideally one USB-A. The passenger with an old cable will thank you. Dual USB-C is sleeker; USB-C plus USB-A is more universally useful in a car.

Build quality and heat behavior. Cars are hot places. A charger that lives in a 60°C cabin needs over-temperature protection — any certified product has it, no-name gas-station specials sometimes do not. A metal body sheds heat better; a tight 12V socket fit prevents the intermittent disconnection that masquerades as "slow charging."

Cable or no cable. Built-in cable chargers are tidy but die with their cable. Separate-port chargers let you replace cables — and cables are what actually break most often. Prefer ports.

What to Ignore

Marketing wattage above 45W for phones. A phone that negotiates 27W cannot be pushed faster by a 120W charger. Extra headroom only matters for multiple devices or laptops.

Every "smart IC" claim. Every certified charger negotiates intelligently; the chip's brand name is not a feature.

LED voltage displays. Fun for a week, useless thereafter, and a light in your eye at night unless it dims.

Quick Charge 2.0/3.0 branding. Legacy protocol for older Androids; modern phones use PD. Compatibility is automatic either way — do not pay extra for the badge.

Special Cases

Electric cars with USB-C ports built in: many deliver real PD wattage, making plug-in chargers redundant for passengers. Check the car's manual for the port's output before buying another gadget.

Motorcycles and commercial fleets: look for rugged, fused chargers with strain-relieved cables; vibration kills consumer models quickly.

Rideshare drivers: a dual-port 65W unit mounted where passengers reach, plus a short cable per port, ends the backseat negotiation. Magnetic tip cables reduce wear on the ports that get cycled dozens of times a day.

A Sensible Default Buy

One dual-port charger — USB-C PD at 30W or better, plus USB-A — from a brand with certifications printed on the body, plus one short (30–50 cm) quality USB-C cable that stays in the car. Total cost is modest, and the combination charges every passenger device at a respectable rate for the life of the car.

Conclusion

A good car charger is a certified PD unit with honest total wattage, one port per regular passenger, and a decent short cable. Heat management and a snug socket fit matter more in a car than on a desk, and marketing wattage beyond your phone's appetite is wasted money. Buy boring and certified; the measured results at [spravs.com](https://spravs.com) show how little separates competent chargers from expensive ones.

FAQ

How many watts does a car charger need for fast phone charging? 20W to 30W with USB PD covers nearly all modern phones at or near full speed. Higher totals matter only when charging multiple devices simultaneously.

Why does my phone charge slowly in the car? Usually the cable, sometimes the port. Old or damaged cables block PD negotiation, and cheap chargers fall back to 5W. A certified PD charger plus a fresh cable fixes most cases.

Are built-in car USB ports good for charging? Varies widely — some car USB ports deliver under 10W. If your built-in ports charge slowly, a PD charger in the 12V socket is the fix, not a new cable.
